What is Maltodextrin — and Why It Has a Higher GI Than Sugar

H
Heather McKenzie · NASM Certified Nutrition Coach
July 8, 2026 5 min read

Maltodextrin appears on the ingredient labels of hundreds of products marketed as healthy — protein powders, granola bars, meal replacements, snack foods, and sugar-free products. Most people scanning labels have never heard of it. And that's precisely the problem.

Because maltodextrin is classified as a carbohydrate — not a sugar — it doesn't appear in the sugar total on nutrition facts panels. You can look at a product, see 2 grams of sugar, feel confident about your choice, and be consuming one of the highest glycemic ingredients in the modern food supply.

⚠️ The Key Fact

Maltodextrin has a glycemic index of 85–105. Table sugar has a glycemic index of 65. Maltodextrin raises blood sugar faster than the sugar you were trying to avoid — and it doesn't count in the sugar total.

What is Maltodextrin?

Maltodextrin is a highly processed carbohydrate derived from starch — typically corn, rice, potato, or wheat starch. During manufacturing, starch is broken down through a process called hydrolysis, producing shorter glucose chains that dissolve easily in water and have a mild, slightly sweet taste.

It's used in processed foods for several reasons:

  • As a bulking agent — adds volume without significant flavor
  • As a thickener — improves texture in powdered products
  • As a filler — extends the volume of protein powders and supplements
  • As a fast-energy source in sports nutrition products
  • As a preservative — extends shelf life of packaged foods

Because it's derived from starch rather than sugar cane or corn syrup, the FDA classifies it as a carbohydrate. This classification means it bypasses sugar labeling requirements — allowing manufacturers to use it as a sweetener and filler while keeping the "sugar" number on the nutrition facts panel artificially low.

The Glycemic Index Problem

The glycemic index (GI) measures how quickly a food raises blood glucose on a scale of 0–100, with pure glucose set at 100 as the reference point.

Maltodextrin's GI of 85–105 means it raises blood sugar as fast as — or faster than — pure glucose. This happens because the short glucose chains are rapidly broken down during digestion and absorbed directly into the bloodstream.

Why This Matters for Label Reading

A protein bar with 5g of sugar and maltodextrin listed as the third ingredient by weight can deliver a blood sugar response comparable to eating pure table sugar — while showing a modest sugar count on the nutrition label.

Where Maltodextrin Hides

Maltodextrin appears across a surprisingly wide range of products — many of them specifically marketed to health-conscious consumers:

  • Protein powders and meal replacements — often listed as the second or third ingredient by weight
  • Granola bars and energy bars — used alongside other sugar sources
  • Sugar-free and diet products — added for bulk and texture after sugar is removed
  • Salad dressings and sauces — used as a thickener
  • Instant puddings and dessert mixes — primary bulking agent
  • Baby formula — some formulations use it as a carbohydrate source
  • Medications and supplements — used as a filler in capsules and tablets
  • Sports drinks and electrolyte powders — as a fast-energy carbohydrate

Is Maltodextrin Ever Appropriate?

The honest answer is: context matters.

Sports nutrition manufacturers add maltodextrin deliberately — because rapid glucose delivery immediately after intense exercise has genuine research support for muscle glycogen replenishment. For an athlete consuming a recovery product within 30 minutes of an intense workout, that rapid glucose spike serves a real purpose.

For everyone else — people using protein powder as a meal replacement, a weight management tool, or a "healthy" snack — the same rapid glucose spike works against their goals entirely.

✅ What to Look For Instead

Products sweetened and bulked with erythritol (GI: 0), allulose (GI: 0), or inulin (GI: very low) deliver the same texture and volume without the glycemic impact. Check the ingredient list — not just the sugar count.

How to Identify Maltodextrin on Labels

Maltodextrin is almost always listed as "maltodextrin" — it doesn't have the same 60+ alias problem that sugar does. But it frequently appears without any indication of its glycemic impact, and its presence in a product is never flagged on the nutrition facts panel.

The most important thing to remember: if maltodextrin appears in the first five ingredients by weight, it's doing significant work in that product. Ingredients are listed in order of quantity — so maltodextrin in position two or three means there's more of it than most other ingredients.

The Bottom Line

Maltodextrin is arguably the most important hidden ingredient to understand when reading food labels — because it sits in a regulatory gap that allows it to affect blood sugar significantly while appearing nowhere in the sugar count.

This is exactly why reading the ingredient list matters more than checking the nutrition facts panel. The numbers on the panel are accurate within their defined categories. But the categories were designed when maltodextrin wasn't in hundreds of health products. The ingredient list tells the complete story.

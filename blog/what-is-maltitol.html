What is Maltitol — and Why It Causes Digestive Issues

H
Heather McKenzie · NASM Certified Nutrition Coach
July 22, 2026 6 min read

You pick up a bag of sugar free candy. The front says "Sugar Free" in large letters. You feel confident about your choice. You eat several pieces.

Then you read the fine print on the back: "Excess consumption may have a laxative effect."

That warning exists because of maltitol — the most common sweetener in sugar free candy, and one of the most misleading ingredients in the modern food supply.

⚠️ The Key Facts

Maltitol has a glycemic index of 35–52 — it still raises blood sugar significantly. It causes bloating, gas, and laxative effects at higher amounts. And it's in the majority of products labeled "sugar free."

What is Maltitol?

Maltitol is a sugar alcohol — a class of sweeteners derived from sugars through a chemical reduction process. It's made by hydrogenating maltose, a sugar derived from starch.

Sugar alcohols occupy a middle ground between sugar and artificial sweeteners. They're not zero-calorie like stevia. They're not fully absorbed like regular sugar. They sit somewhere in between — and that middle ground creates both their appeal to food manufacturers and their problems for consumers.

Maltitol is popular in sugar free products because:

  • It behaves similarly to sugar in baking and confectionery — same texture, browning, and mouthfeel
  • It's about 75–90% as sweet as sugar
  • It has fewer calories than sugar (approximately 2.1 calories per gram vs 4 for sugar)
  • It allows manufacturers to legally label products as "sugar free"
  • It's significantly cheaper than better sugar alternatives like erythritol or allulose

The Glycemic Index Problem

The most important thing to understand about maltitol is that "sugar free" does not mean "blood sugar friendly."

Maltitol's GI of 35–52 means it raises blood sugar at roughly half the rate of table sugar. For someone managing blood sugar carefully — a diabetic, a person with insulin resistance, or anyone following a low glycemic diet — this is a meaningful impact that the "sugar free" label completely obscures.

Why This Is Particularly Relevant for Diabetics

Products labeled "diabetic friendly" or "suitable for diabetics" frequently use maltitol as the primary sweetener. The FDA does not regulate these claims. A product can legally use both labels while containing a sweetener that still affects blood sugar significantly.

Why Maltitol Causes Digestive Issues

This is the part most people discover the hard way.

Maltitol is only partially absorbed in the small intestine. The portion that isn't absorbed passes into the large intestine — where it becomes food for gut bacteria. This fermentation process produces gas, which causes bloating and flatulence.

At higher amounts maltitol acts as an osmotic laxative — drawing water into the intestine and accelerating transit, causing diarrhea.

The threshold varies by individual but research suggests digestive symptoms can begin at 20–30 grams of maltitol. A single serving of some sugar free chocolate products contains 15–20 grams of maltitol — meaning two or three pieces can trigger symptoms in sensitive individuals.

This is why the laxative warning appears on the packaging. It's not a precautionary statement — it's a documented effect of the primary ingredient.

Maltitol vs Better Sugar Alcohols

Not all sugar alcohols are equal. Maltitol is at the problematic end of the spectrum:

✅ Better Sugar Alcohol Options

Erythritol (GI: 0) — absorbed in the small intestine and excreted unchanged. Minimal digestive effects even at higher amounts. The best tolerated sugar alcohol available.

Xylitol (GI: 13) — lower GI than maltitol, moderate digestive tolerance. Common in dental products for its cavity-fighting properties.

Allulose (GI: 0) — technically a rare sugar not a sugar alcohol, but functions similarly. Excellent digestive tolerance and zero glycemic impact.

When you scan a sugar free product and see erythritol or allulose as the primary sweetener — that's a genuinely good sign. When you see maltitol — especially as the first or second ingredient — you're looking at a product with both a meaningful glycemic impact and potential digestive consequences.

Where Maltitol Hides

  • Sugar free chocolate and candy — the most common source
  • Sugar free cookies and baked goods — provides the texture sugar normally would
  • Diabetic friendly products — used specifically because of the lower GI vs sugar
  • Sugar free protein bars — alongside other sweeteners
  • Sugar free ice cream and frozen desserts — maintains texture at lower temperatures
  • Cough drops and throat lozenges — common in sugar free versions

How to Read Sugar Free Labels

When you pick up a sugar free product the most important thing to check is not the sugar count — it's which sweetener replaced the sugar.

Here's a quick reference for what you want to see vs what to be cautious about:

  • ✅ Erythritol — GI 0, excellent tolerance
  • ✅ Allulose — GI 0, excellent tolerance
  • ✅ Stevia leaf extract — GI 0, excellent tolerance
  • ✅ Monk fruit extract — GI 0, excellent tolerance
  • ⚠️ Xylitol — GI 13, moderate tolerance
  • 🚩 Maltitol — GI 35–52, poor tolerance at higher amounts
  • 🚩 Sorbitol — GI 9, significant laxative effect
  • 🚩 Mannitol — GI 2, significant digestive effects

The Bottom Line

Maltitol is the sugar free sweetener that delivers the worst of both worlds — it still affects blood sugar meaningfully, and it causes digestive discomfort at the amounts typically consumed from sugar free products.

The "sugar free" label tells you one thing: no sucrose was used. It tells you nothing about what replaced it, how that replacement affects your blood sugar, or what happens to your digestive system when you consume it in normal amounts.

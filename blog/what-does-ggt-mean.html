What is GGT?

GGT stands for Gamma-Glutamyl Transferase (sometimes written Gamma-Glutamyl Transpeptidase). It's an enzyme found primarily in the liver and bile ducts, with smaller amounts in the kidneys, pancreas, and other organs.

GGT has two primary functions in the body:

  • Amino acid transport — helping move amino acids across cell membranes, particularly in the liver and kidneys
  • Glutathione metabolism — playing a key role in recycling glutathione, your body's primary antioxidant

When liver cells are under stress they release GGT into the bloodstream. This is why elevated GGT on a blood test is a signal — it means something is causing stress to your liver cells.

What is a Normal GGT Level?

Normal GGT ranges vary slightly between laboratories but the generally accepted reference range is:

What Causes Elevated GGT?

GGT elevates in response to a wide range of liver stressors. The most commonly discussed causes include:

  • Alcohol consumption — GGT is one of the most sensitive markers for alcohol-related liver stress
  • Medications — many common medications including statins, antiepileptics, and some antibiotics can elevate GGT
  • Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) — now renamed metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD)
  • Bile duct obstruction — blockages in the bile duct system elevate GGT significantly
  • Fructose and hidden sugar consumption — this is the cause most commonly overlooked in clinical practice

The Hidden Sugar Connection — Why This Is Under-Discussed

Fructose is metabolized almost exclusively by the liver. Unlike glucose — which is processed by every cell in the body — fructose goes directly to the liver regardless of how much is consumed or what your energy needs are.

When fructose consumption is high and consistent, the following cascade occurs:

  • The liver converts excess fructose to fat through de novo lipogenesis — the beginning of fatty liver
  • Fructose metabolism generates uric acid — an inflammatory compound that stresses liver cells
  • Oxidative stress increases in the liver as it works to process the fructose load
  • GGT activity increases to manage that oxidative stress — because GGT is directly involved in glutathione recycling
  • GGT spills into the bloodstream — showing up as elevated on a blood test

This is why GGT often elevates from dietary fructose before other liver markers like ALT or AST show any abnormality. GGT is the canary in the coal mine — the early warning signal that liver stress is occurring.

Why "Your Other Markers Are Normal" Isn't Enough

ALT and AST — the markers most doctors focus on for liver damage — tend to elevate later in the progression of metabolic liver stress. GGT often elevates years earlier. A normal ALT and AST alongside elevated GGT doesn't mean "nothing is wrong." It may mean you're at an early stage where intervention would be most effective.

The Problem With Hidden Sugars and GGT

The fructose driving GGT elevation often isn't coming from obvious sugar sources. It's coming from hidden sugars in foods marketed as healthy, natural, and organic:

  • Agave nectar — 60–90% fructose, used widely in health food products
  • High fructose corn syrup — 55% fructose, in sauces, dressings, breads
  • Fruit juice concentrate — concentrated fructose in baby food, juices, smoothies
  • Brown rice syrup — rapidly converted to glucose then fructose, GI of 98
  • Invert cane syrup — pre-digested fructose and glucose

Someone eating what they genuinely believe is a healthy diet — organic granola bars, natural energy bars, healthy juices, clean protein powders — may be consuming significant fructose daily from these hidden sources. Their GGT reflects this. But without knowing to connect the label to the lab result, the connection is invisible.

How to Get Your GGT Tested

GGT is typically included in a comprehensive metabolic panel (CMP) — a standard blood test ordered by most doctors. If your doctor hasn't mentioned GGT specifically, ask to see your result on your next lab report.

What to Do If Your GGT Is Elevated

First — consult with your doctor. Elevated GGT has multiple possible causes and medical evaluation is important.

Second — look at your diet. Specifically look at the fructose sources you're consuming daily without recognizing them as sugar. Scan the ingredient labels of the foods you eat every day at sugarscannerapp.com — look for agave, fruit juice concentrate, high fructose corn syrup, brown rice syrup, and invert cane syrup in the ingredient list.

Third — track your numbers over time. A single GGT result tells you where you are today. Multiple results over months tell you whether your interventions are working.

✅ The Takeaway

GGT is one of the most valuable and most underused markers for metabolic health available on a standard blood panel. If yours is elevated — even mildly — it's worth investigating what fructose sources you're consuming daily before accepting "don't worry about it" as the final answer.

The Bottom Line

GGT is not just a liver enzyme. It's a metabolic health signal — one that often elevates from dietary fructose consumption years before other liver markers show any abnormality.

Knowing what your GGT is, what it means, and what dietary factors drive it is one of the most actionable pieces of health information available from a standard blood test.

And knowing what's in your food — specifically which hidden sugars contain fructose and in what amounts — is the first step toward understanding what your GGT is telling you.
